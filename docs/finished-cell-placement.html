Where each cell goes

Four placement rules decide the position of every split cell in the Finished view — one for the splitter's run, one for the cord's run, one for the column, one for a cord returning to the surface. This consolidates them, with the geometry each one actually asserts.

The cell, and the names its corners carry

One split event makes one parallelogram. Its two vertical sides sit on the two boundaries of its gap column; the splitter's crossing line runs corner to corner between them. Every placement rule below is stated in terms of these four points, so they are worth pinning down first.

The packed tip angle

At the default tipAngle = 30° each cell is a long sliver — that is what the figures draw. tipAngle = 2θ is the special value: all four sides come out equal, the cell is a rhombus, and the surface tiles exactly. Between the two, only the height of each contact changes, never which cells touch.

R1

A connected splitter meets corner to corner

provisional

spec §7 · buildFinishedLayout, the per-action candidate loop

One written action — 1>2,3,4 — is one physical splitter passing through three splittees in the same direction. It produces three cells in three consecutive columns, and they are laid out on a single unbroken line: the point where the splitter's crossing diagonal leaves one cell is the point where it enters the next.

Because consecutive cells of an action sit in different columns, they share only their common boundary line — and on that line each takes a disjoint interval, the earlier cell above the contact point and the later cell below it. So the contact is exactly one point: corner to corner, never an edge and never an overlap.

event start   = previous event end
event end     = (start.x ± columnWidth, start.y + crossGapDrop)
start.y       = actionBaseline ± eventIndex · crossGapDrop

Why provisional. R1 builds the action's scaffold before any cord is consulted. Alignment (R2) then translates each cell by its own anchor's offset, so an action carrying two cords whose ends sit at different heights bends: its corner contacts open up. That is intended — a cord's edge join is exact, an action's corner join is where the action starts.

R2

A connected cord shares a whole edge

exact anchor

spec §7.1 · alignContinuingSplittees

The other run in the surface belongs to a cord, not to an action. When the same stable cord is the splittee of two events in adjacent columns, its two cells share the boundary between those columns — and there they must coincide along their complete edge, both endpoints, not merely overlap or touch at a point.

The join is made by aligning one pair of corners: the previous cell's outgoing top corner (at its fromLane) onto the new cell's incoming top corner (at its toLane). Since every vertical side is cellSide long, matching the top corners matches the bottom ones too.

Three things break the run, and each starts a new one rather than bending this rule:

  • Acting as a splitter. The cord's trajectory goes invisible; its previous visible end is dropped as a constraint and is never bridged by paint.
  • Returning through the same gap. Two cells on the same side of one boundary would overlap interiors if their edges were equated. They stack under R3 instead.
  • Nothing else. Not a row boundary, a repeat, a colour match, a change of splitter, or a Front/Back switch. Continuity is by stable cord ID.

Every anchored cell keeps its own offset — offsets are never averaged across a row. Only cells with no anchor of their own are interpolated between the nearest anchors on either side, and beyond the last anchor the nearest offset simply extends.

R3

Inside one column, the lean decides

solver

spec §7.3 · packConnectedRibbons

Two cells in the same gap column are governed by their split directions, and by nothing else — not by their cords, their colours, their event order, or whether either is a role transition.

Same lean — contact is compulsory

Same-leaning cells in a column must touch: the lower cell's top edge coincides with the upper cell's bottom edge across the column's full width, with no vertical gap and no interior overlap. An intervening opposite-leaning course does not break that chain — it passes across those cells rather than separating them — so each cell meets the previous cell of its own lean in that column, edge to edge. A gap there is a hole in the surface, not room for an unsplit cord.

Opposite lean — spacing is inherited, not chosen

A left-leaning and a right-leaning cell in one column may partially overlap, or stand well apart leaving open space for cords with no split event there. There is no pitch, no maximum gap, and no requirement to close the space. Do not add a clearance constraint, do not force the later event below the earlier one, and do not pull them together: that space is where a cord passes without splitting, and closing it detaches the joins R2 made.

Free is not arbitrary. How much overlap or space appears is determined — by the other rules, not this one. Each of the two cells has already been placed by its own run: R1's scaffold puts it on its splitter's line, R2 pins it to the cord it continues, R4 pins a returning cord to its exit. The distance between them at that boundary is simply what those placements left. So it is an output of the layout and fully reproducible: to predict or check a gap, track each cell back to the anchor that fixed its run and take the difference of the two runs' offsets at the shared boundary. R3 contributes nothing to an opposite-lean pair precisely so that both cords' own positions survive intact.

Because these constraints chain between runs, they are solved together rather than swept: a pass that only pushes cells downward until overlaps disappear satisfies neither the equalities nor the joins. Each connected ribbon run takes one common vertical offset — earlier cells included — and the canvas bounds are normalized after.

R4

A cord changing roles stays next to its own neighbour

anchor where free

spec §7.2.1 · returningCentre, applied in alignContinuingSplittees and held through packConnectedRibbons

When a cord changes role between two adjacent columns, its two cells belong beside each other. The offset is not a free choice: the later cell's top corner on their shared boundary lands on the centre of the earlier cell's side — the vertical edge that cell has on that boundary. The later cell therefore sits exactly half a cellSide lower, interlocking with the earlier one's lower half, however many unrelated events ran in between.

The rule is symmetric, because a split-event transition is (§2.2). A cord that stops splitting comes back to the surface; a cord that was visible leaves it. Both are one cord crossing one boundary at its own lane, so both take the same anchor — only the edge each cell offers to that boundary differs.

Both cells always lean opposite ways: for their columns to be adjacent, the two events' other lanes must sit on opposite sides of the cord's own lane. So R3 leaves this pair's own spacing free by construction, and the anchor is what decides it.

Where it yields. The two cells are rarely alone: each belongs to a run that R2's cord joins and R3's column contacts may have already tied to the other. When they have, the role change and those contacts cannot both hold — asserting both makes the constraint system contradictory, and the solver says so rather than picking silently. So each role change is admitted only while the whole system still resolves; one that cannot be held keeps the placement its alignment gave it, and never reopens a cord join, a column contact or an interior overlap to make room for itself. This is the same principle R3 states from the other side: an exact rule claims the freedom the other rules left, and never overwrites what they already settled.

Coverage

Every role change that takes the anchor lands on the centre exactly. Measured over all nine angle combinations: the chevron and the colour block take 7 of 7 returns, the double chevron 14 of 14 returns and 7 of 7 departures. The denser samples share the freedom out — the wayuu sample takes 39% of its 29 returns and 70% of its 28 departures, the mirrored diamonds 67% and 33%, braid16 37% and 33%.

The wayuu sample's own regression is the departure the rule was missing: C08 splits C02 in column 4, then C02 splits C09 in column 5. Before the departure anchor, the second cell sat 404.1 below the first at columnWidth 100 with a 173.2 hole between them; it now meets the centre at every angle.

How the four are resolved

  1. Scaffold each action on its splitter's line (R1). Split the events into actions by row instance and splitter, pick the course direction — descending, or ascending when the existing cord ends fit that better — and lay the action's cells corner to corner from there.
  2. Anchor the continuing cords (R2). Each cell whose splittee was a splittee before, one column over, takes the exact offset that puts its incoming top corner on the previous cell's outgoing top corner. Unanchored cells interpolate between neighbours. R4's return anchor is applied here too: same step, same machinery, offset by half a cellSide instead of zero.
  3. Solve the columns together (R3). Group adjacent-gap appearances of a cord into runs; state one equality per consecutive same-leaning pair and a minimum separation for the rest; propagate until the offsets settle, and translate each whole run — never a single cell away from its neighbours. Cells tied by an equality form one rigid body, and R4's role changes are offered last, one at a time: each is kept only if the whole system still resolves with it, and skipped otherwise.
  4. Normalize. Translate everything into the padded canvas and report the bounds.

Order matters in one direction only: a later step may move a whole run, never break a join an earlier step made exact.

Position invariants

The subset of §8 that a placement change can break:

  • Each cell's crossing diagonal ends on its two column boundaries, before any action-level translation.
  • Within one action, consecutive cells meet at one point on the splitter's line — no overlap, no gap — unless an R2 anchor has bent the action.
  • A cord's consecutive splittee cells in adjacent gaps align both endpoints of their common boundary, including across course changes and at identical colours.
  • Each cell meets the previous cell of its own lean in its column along a complete top-to-bottom edge — no gap, no interior overlap — including across intervening opposite-leaning courses, role-transition cells and same-gap returns.
  • Opposite-leaning cells in a column may overlap or leave space, by the amount their runs' anchors leave; packing never closes that space.
  • Every ribbon run receives one common vertical offset.
  • A cord's invisible splitter portion is never filled to connect two visible cells.
  • A cord changing roles one column over puts the later cell's top corner on the centre of the earlier cell's side, in both directions and whatever happened in between — unless a cord join or a column contact has already fixed both cells.
  • cellSide depends only on θ and tipAngle — never on the event or its column.
  • One cell per split event, at every supported angle, on both faces.
